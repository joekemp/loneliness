Then four reasons no counter-culture formed:

 1. Release was licensed and bounded. Gluckman's "rituals of rebellion," Bakhtin's carnival, Turner's liminality — these reaffirm structure rather than reverse it. The pub literally rings a bell to end the exception. Licence with a closing time is a safety valve, and a safety valve is part of the machine.                                                                                        
 2. The Reformation abolished institutional catharsis. Catholic Europe kept confession, the wake, the keen, the ritual lament, the saint cult — theatres of    emotion with a place and a form. England dissolved them. The route to collective emotional expression was not merely discouraged; it was de-institutionalised. Keith Thomas's Religion and the Decline of Magic and Peter Burke's Popular Culture in Early Modern Europe trace the demolition of that apparatus.

 3. The residual expressive form is defensive, not disclosive. Irony, self-deprecation, banter, understatement — these protect the self rather than 
 reveal it. Even the culture's expressiveness is armour.

 4. Alcohol produces congruence, not revelation. Drink gives shared sentiment for an evening; it does not disclose the sustained inner life that friendship requires. The morning reasserts the reserve. It relieves the symptom and leaves the disease.
                                                                           
The proof that the capacity exists but needs permission is mourning. Diana's flowers, the Armistice silence, the pandemic clapping: licensed collective effervescence that surprises the British themselves. The emotion is present;  routine channel is absent. (The other British exemption is animals — we are unrestrained with pets and largely with children. That's not a joke; it tells you the reserve is a rule about adult humans, not a temperament.)
