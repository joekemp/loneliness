Britain runs on a grammar of negative politeness — non-imposition, privacy, self-deprecation, and the prohibition on earnestness — which reliably produces a  high density of pleasant acquaintances and a scarcity of intimate friendship. Kate Fox's Watching the English is the closest thing to an ethnography of this: she describes English sociality as anxiety managed by elaborate rules, and notes how "taking the piss" functions as both affection and a pre-emptive defence against sincerity. That last mechanism matters most for you: banter lets two people be very close without ever risking vulnerability, so relationships plateau at a level that feels warm but never becomes a friendship in Aristotle's sense. Add the pub-and-alcohol dependency (the lubricant for an otherwise forbidden expressiveness), and you have a culture that can be friendly for decades without becoming intimate. That's not a bug people experience as a deficiency; it's a design feature they mistake for normalcy.
