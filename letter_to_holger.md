# No Drama
### A letter on argument, belonging, and being known

*26 September 2026, Newcastle*

*Based on our conversation of last night, 25 September 2026, and the thinking since. Some of the prose was shaped with an AI; the argument is mine, and I'll stand behind it. Numbers in brackets link to the full entries in `references.md`. The long historical scaffolding has been kept aside in `history.md`; read it with suspicion.*

Holger,

As you know, a year ago I lost a relationship and, with it, an entire network that came attached to it — her friends, her family, the ordinary furniture of a shared life. I had assumed there was something underneath that would catch me. There wasn't. We agreed last night to each write down our thoughts on what we discussed. What follows is mine. It is a problem statement, not a proposal: I have tried to describe the thing before reaching for a cure.

I came back from six years in Germany almost a quarter of a century ago. In that time I have not made a single close British friend. I am, by your own account, your only British friend, and you are, by any measure, mine; your other friends here are all from somewhere else. We keep arriving at the same question from opposite directions. We have each spent years in Germany and years in England, so we both see it, from both ends — which is why I'm writing to you rather than to anyone else.

Here is the claim I want to defend. British loneliness is not primarily a defect of individuals, and it is not only a shortage of places. It is the joint product of a culture that has made disagreement unsafe and of the disappearance of the places where disagreement could have been survived — two causes that feed each other. A culture that treats friction itself as a failure of manners removes the one practice that turns acquaintance into intimacy. Intimacy is not the absence of friction; it is the practice of surviving friction together. A country that forbids the friction has cut the wire that deepens relationships. Its friendships plateau at exactly the point where the first real disagreement would have occurred.

Here is how the two causes feed each other, because the mechanism matters. Safe friction needs something to be safe *inside*. It cannot be the friendship, because the friendship is the thing being made. It has to be a prior membership — a role, a place, a team, a street — a belonging that the disagreement cannot revoke. That is what the pub, the chapel, the union, the *Verein* and the office used to supply: not intimacy, but a container in which intimacy could be risked. Belonging is the safety; disclosure is the conversion; friction is the proof. Belonging without being known is not the failure. It is the raw material. The failure is that we dismantled the container and then forbade the risk.

Strip the container down and what it actually supplied was not exemption from friction but *repair*: the shared membership, the obligation, the knowledge that you would both be at the same bar next week, the route back after a bad night. Friction is not something a culture permits or forbids; it is simply what happens when people are actually together. What varies is whether the setting can mend it. So the two halves of this argument are not two separate failures. They are different in origin and meet at the same joint — both are deficits of repair — and they are coupled rather than parallel. Remove the local places and friction does not stop; it moves to the only setting left, the virtual, which has no next week and no route back. The fear of unrepaired friction then makes us avoid it, and avoidance stops us rebuilding the local places that would do the repairing. Two causes, one loop.

I still can't separate how much of this is me and how much is England — and I've started to suspect that the inability to separate them is the thing I'm describing. Englishness works by making a cultural rule feel like a personal failing. Which is the worst possible arrangement, because a personal failing is something you apologise for, and a cultural rule is something you could argue with.

When friction is forbidden, people take one of two exits, and both leave the middle ground empty. They avoid it — they become apolitical, undemanding, allergic to drama; there is a reason "no drama" has become a genre of dating profiles. Or they entrench — they take a side, treat the disagreement as a war of identities, and cannot come back. Avoidance and entrenchment look like opposites. They are the same failure. Between the two there was supposed to be a third thing: disagreement that stays safe, that does not cost you the relationship. That is the thing we have lost.

The distinction I keep coming back to, and the whole reason any of this matters, is between **belonging** and **being known**. *Belonging* is integration into a collective — class, place, nation, club, union. *Being known* is the reciprocal, particular, disclosed knowledge of one person by another. The evidence is that these two are only loosely connected, and that loneliness tracks the second, not the first: you can be socially integrated and still lonely [[12]](references.md#ref-12 "Cacioppo, John T., and William Patrick. Loneliness. New York: W. W. Norton, 2008.")[[13]](references.md#ref-13 "Hawkley, Louise C., and John T. Cacioppo. &quot;Loneliness Matters.&quot; Annals of Behavioral Medicine 40, no. 2 (2010): 218–227.")[[14]](references.md#ref-14 "Holt-Lunstad, Julianne, et al. &quot;Loneliness and Social Isolation as Risk Factors for Mortality.&quot; Perspectives on Psychological Science 10, no. 2 (2015): 227–237."). Loneliness itself is a historically specific emotion, not a timeless one [[16]](references.md#ref-16 "Bound Alberti, Fay. A Biography of Loneliness. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2019."), and it is better understood as a signal — a hunger — than as a simple absence of people [[9]](references.md#ref-9 "Svendsen, Lars. A Philosophy of Loneliness. Trans. Kerri Pierce. London: Reaktion, 2017.")[[12]](references.md#ref-12 "Cacioppo, John T., and William Patrick. Loneliness. New York: W. W. Norton, 2008."). Britain, on my reading, was historically rich in the first thing and poor in the second.

And there is a consequence I want to state plainly, because it governs everything that might come after. This is not a story of loss and restoration. There is no golden age to return to. *Belonging* was historically rich here; *being known* was never institutionalised at all. So whatever the remedy turns out to be, it is construction, not restoration — and nostalgia is not a route.

I am not bad at arguing. I am bad at *disagreeing* — the ordinary business of two people wanting different things and staying in the room. I had quietly arranged a lot of my life around not entering rooms where that might happen. The mere possibility of friction was enough to keep me out of the conversation. I've since read up on disagreeing well — listening, separating the person from the position, repairing afterwards [[107]](references.md#ref-107 "Atkins, Ros. The Art of Explanation: How to Communicate with Clarity and Confidence. London: Wildfire, 2023.")[[108]](references.md#ref-108 "Minson, Julia. How to Disagree Better. London: Orion Ignite, 2026.")[[109]](references.md#ref-109 "Stone, Douglas, Bruce Patton, and Sheila Heen. Difficult Conversations: How to Discuss What Matters Most. New York: Viking, 1999.") — and the anxiety has eased a lot.

I know this from the inside, and not only from the country. I learned before I could name it that keeping the peace meant not having a position — a playbook I watched my mother run on my father, and then ran myself. Later I took the other fork as well, and lost my temper when the peace had gone on too long. I have been the one who disappears to avoid the argument and the one who explodes when it finally arrives. Neither made me a friend. It took me a long time to see that the two were the same mistake: both treated friction as danger rather than information.

As I said last night, and have said many times before: Germany was a relief for exactly this reason. No offence was taken at having a point of view. You could talk to a German about anything — politics, money, your own failures — and it landed as conversation rather than as a demand or an insult. You disagree more easily than any English person I know, and I don't think you are unusual in that. And I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to turn you into a parable. Germany is lonely too; the government there now runs a loneliness barometer, and the rates are not low [[117]](references.md#ref-117 "Federal Ministry for Family Affairs, Senior Citizens, Women and Youth (BMFSFJ). Einsamkeitsbarometer. Berlin, 2024."). The difference is not warmth, and it is not temperament. It is that German disagreement has *formats*: the *Verein*, the *Stammtisch*, the seminar, the café argument. Friction has a home, a role, and a set of manners. So a German can say the true thing at the table and still be at the table next week. We have the same emotion and nowhere to put it. You buy a pint and you rent a stool; there is no membership, no obligation, and no door. Germany's reserve is a wall with a door. England's friendliness is a door with no room behind it.

Start with the pub, since that's where we were last night. Picture the bell, not the beer: everything that makes a pub good is cancelled by the bell. The unforced repetition — the same faces at the same bar on the same night — is the raw material of friendship [[21]](references.md#ref-21 "Zajonc, Robert B. &quot;Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure.&quot; Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 9 (1968): 1–27.")[[22]](references.md#ref-22 "Granovetter, Mark S. &quot;The Strength of Weak Ties.&quot; American Journal of Sociology 78, no. 6 (1973): 1360–1380.")[[23]](references.md#ref-23 "Dunbar, Robin. Friends. London: Little, Brown, 2021.")[[24]](references.md#ref-24 "Oldenburg, Ray. The Great Good Place. New York: Paragon House, 1989."). Oldenburg called such places *third places*: neither home nor work, where contact accumulates without ever being scheduled [[24]](references.md#ref-24 "Oldenburg, Ray. The Great Good Place. New York: Paragon House, 1989."). The pub supplied that at national scale. And then it rang a bell and told you when the belonging stopped.

Notice what the pub did produce. Not sentimental evenings — friendly societies, trade unions, the co-op, Methodist class meetings. Real, durable, collective things [[42]](references.md#ref-42 "Hoggart, Richard. The Uses of Literacy. London: Chatto & Windus, 1957.")[[44]](references.md#ref-44 "Thompson, E. P. The Making of the English Working Class. London: Victor Gollancz, 1963."). What it did not produce, in anything like the same way, was *being known*. It produced *solidarity*. Solidarity is collective and role-based; intimacy is one-to-one and self-revealing. You can be lonely inside a dense solidarity, and Hoggart's working class is the proof: enormous loyalty, almost no self-revelation [[42]](references.md#ref-42 "Hoggart, Richard. The Uses of Literacy. London: Chatto & Windus, 1957.").

Underneath it all is a grammar. Fox's account of Englishness is a set of rules for managing anxiety: non-imposition, privacy, self-deprecation, and a prohibition on earnestness [[37]](references.md#ref-37 "Fox, Kate. Watching the English. London: Hodder & Stoughton, 2004."). "Taking the piss" is the hinge of it — it is affection, and it is also a pre-emptive defence against sincerity. It lets two people be close without either of them ever risking vulnerability. Goffman would say we are all front stage [[25]](references.md#ref-25 "Goffman, Erving. The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life. New York: Doubleday Anchor, 1959."); the pub offers the performance of intimacy without the back stage. Alcohol helps, in the specific way that makes the problem invisible: it produces congruence — a shared mood for an evening — but it does not disclose the sustained inner life. The morning reasserts the reserve. It relieves the symptom and leaves the disease. You and I don't need the pub or the drink for this — we've had these conversations while making a coffee at work while sober, and they come out much the same. But most of Britain seems to need the licence. We are the exception that shows how the rule works.

The proof that the capacity exists, and merely lacks permission, is mourning. The flowers for Diana, the Armistice silence, the clapping for the NHS: licensed "collective effervescence" that surprises the British themselves [[29]](references.md#ref-29 "Durkheim, Émile. The Elementary Forms of Religious Life. 1912."). The emotion is present. The routine channel is absent. The other exemption is dogs — and specifically the intensity so many women bring to them, on display for anyone to see on a dating profile. Is it what you reach for when you have learned not to expect intimacy from men? If so, it isn't a quirk. It is the reserve being worked around — and it tells you, once more, that the reserve is a rule about adult humans rather than a temperament.

The country I grew up in does not merely discourage complaint; it treats friction itself as a failure of manners. *Don't make a scene. Don't cause a fuss. I might be wrong, but.* And here it meets the argument. You made this point yourself last night — that friendship is the one relationship in which disagreement is safe. Aristotle had it first: the highest friendship, virtue-friendship, as against friendships of use or of pleasure, requires time, equality, and the willingness to wish the other well enough to tell them the truth [[3]](references.md#ref-3 "Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Books VIII–IX. Trans. Terence Irwin. Indianapolis: Hackett, 1999."). Cicero says the essence of friendship is candour: better to be told the truth by a friend than flattered [[4]](references.md#ref-4 "Cicero. Laelius de Amicitia (On Friendship). 44 BCE."). Montaigne, of his one perfect friendship, wrote simply that it was because it was he, because it was I [[10]](references.md#ref-10 "Montaigne, Michel de. &quot;Of Friendship.&quot; In Essays, 1580."). All three treat friendship as the relationship in which disagreement is safe. The line usually attributed to Voltaire — that he would defend to the death your right to say what he disapproved of — is actually Evelyn Beatrice Hall's, and it is the whole creed: disagreement without exit [[116]](references.md#ref-116 "Hall, Evelyn Beatrice (as S. G. Tallentyre). The Friends of Voltaire. London: John Murray, 1906.").

The psychology agrees. Intimacy is built by disclosure and responsiveness: we tell each other true things, and the other receives them without punishment, and that exchange is what turns acquaintance into closeness [[110]](references.md#ref-110 "Altman, Irwin, and Dalmas A. Taylor. Social Penetration: The Development of Interpersonal Relationships. New York: Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1973.")[[111]](references.md#ref-111 "Reis, Harry T., and Phillip Shaver. &quot;Intimacy as an Interpersonal Process.&quot; In Handbook of Personal Relationships, edited by Steve Duck. Chichester: Wiley, 1988.")[[112]](references.md#ref-112 "Laurenceau, Jean-Philippe, Lisa Feldman Barrett, and Paula R. Pietromonaco. &quot;Intimacy as an Interpersonal Process.&quot; Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 74, no. 5 (1998): 1238–1251."). Vulnerability is the currency — not because suffering is noble, but because showing someone something unguarded and not being dismissed for it is how trust is minted (the popular version is Brené Brown's [[113]](references.md#ref-113 "Brown, Brené. Daring Greatly. New York: Gotham Books, 2012.")). Recognition is the social form it takes: to be disagreed with seriously is to be met as a person, not managed as a problem. Which means a culture with a taboo on friction has removed the test — and a friendship that is never tested is never known.

We also changed the licence without changing the culture. We extended the opening hours and thought we were building a café society; we just got pissed until later. Permission is not culture. You can legalise the thing and still not have the place, the habit, or the people.

There is a harder reason the middle ground stays empty, and it is about who pays for using it. The middle register — sober, verbal, unhurried, uncompetitive — costs status. Certain pursuits are marked as effeminate or "gay"; the working class is held to a narrow approved script, visible in something as trivial and as policed as a dress code; the middle class has its own approved script, which is irony. And the most heavily policed register of all is masculine expressiveness. The stiff upper lip is the taboo's poster child: the public-school ethos, the vulgarised Stoicism read out of Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus, the imperial ideal of enduring and understating [[71]](references.md#ref-71 "Mangan, J. A. Athleticism in the Victorian and Edwardian Public School. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1981.")[[72]](references.md#ref-72 "Vance, Norman. The Sinews of the Spirit. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1985.")[[75]](references.md#ref-75 "Marcus Aurelius. Meditations.")[[76]](references.md#ref-76 "Epictetus. Discourses."). Men are not the only ones under the rule — women get a different one, the injunction to be nice, to smooth rather than disagree — but both rules forbid the same thing. Neither lets you be known.

Then there is what the digital age did to all of it. Cancel culture is not a new disease. It is the entrenchment fork made total: if disagreement cannot be repaired, the only remaining move is expulsion. What is new is the technology — the permanent record, the networked audience, the sentence that follows you — which makes expulsion cheap and inescapable. And so the trap tightens: the taboo produces the punishment, the punishment raises the cost of disclosure, and the raised cost produces more avoidance and more entrenchment. People sort into camps, and the camps are warm. That is the cruel trick of it. A camp gives you belonging at exactly the moment it takes away being known: you are loved for your side and known by no one. Sartre called the move underneath it bad faith: treat someone as their identity and you no longer have to meet them [[114]](references.md#ref-114 "Sartre, Jean-Paul. Being and Nothingness. 1943. Trans. Hazel E. Barnes. New York: Philosophical Library, 1956.")[[115]](references.md#ref-115 "Sartre, Jean-Paul. Existentialism Is a Humanism. 1946. Trans. Carol Macomber. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2007."). This runs in every direction. The anti-woke and the woke, the Leaver and the Remainer, each camp has its own unsayables, and everyone has learned to read the room before they speak. That fluency is the disease.

None of this would be fatal if the reserve still had something to soften it. It doesn't. The infrastructure is gone or behind a paywall. Deindustrialisation ended the institutions that grew around work [[83]](references.md#ref-83 "Todd, Selina. The People. London: John Murray, 2014."). A politics arrived that could say, without embarrassment, that there is no such thing as society. Suburbia and the commute replaced the walkable street, and after 2010 austerity finished the remaining social infrastructure — libraries, Sure Start, youth services, community centres — while roughly a quarter of the pubs closed anyway [[80]](references.md#ref-80 "Klinenberg, Eric. Palaces for the People. New York: Crown, 2018.")[[82]](references.md#ref-82 "Dorling, Danny. All That Is Solid. London: Allen Lane, 2014.").

And there is a resource more basic than place, which the same decades enclosed: time. The repetition that turns acquaintance into friendship needs unscheduled, undemanding hours, and those have been rationed [[78]](references.md#ref-78 "Putnam, Robert D. Bowling Alone. New York: Simon & Schuster, 2000."). The commute, the two-earner household, the gig shift, the exhaustion of work and home — all of it means that even where people still want the friendship, they no longer have the hours or the energy to spend on it. Intimacy cannot be assembled in the forty-five minutes left at the end of a working day, with the phone already demanding the next thing.

And you can see where the time went by looking at who is left in the rooms. The clubs that survive are increasingly the preserve of the retired — you and I have signed up to several and found them full of pensioners and almost empty of people our own age. That is not a complaint about the pensioners; they are simply the ones with the free afternoons. It is the time budget made visible in a demographic. The institution still stands; it has just been vacated by everyone who still has to work. We did not only lose the third place. We lost the empty afternoon.

And where the market notices the shortage, it sells the remedy back to us: the pay-to-play friendship economy — the app, the mixer, the dinner at £145 a head — running the numbers again (15% of Britons with no close friend, 8% with none at all) [[105]](references.md#ref-105 "Hunt, Elle. &quot;We're in a crisis of loneliness – but is buying friends on an app likely to help solve it?&quot; The Guardian, 24 September 2026. https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2026/sep/24/crisis-loneliness-buying-friends-on-an-app-shortcuts."). Payment can create the conditions for friendship; it cannot manufacture the friendship itself. The casual friendship — the low-stakes, no-outcome, see-what-happens kind — has no box on the form, because intention kills it.

The policy response makes all of this worse by misnaming it, treating a structural production as an individual deficit to be coached [[84]](references.md#ref-84 "HM Government. A Connected Society: A Strategy for Tackling Loneliness. London: DCMS, 2018.")[[86]](references.md#ref-86 "Bickerdike, Liz, et al. &quot;Social Prescribing: Less Rhetoric and More Reality.&quot; BMJ Open 7 (2017): e013384.")[[87]](references.md#ref-87 "Rose, Nikolas. Powers of Freedom. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1999."). The long version of that history is in `history.md`. The only thing I need it to settle, it settles: the reserve is made, not given, and so is the disappearance of everything that used to soften it. Neither is a national essence. Both have a history — and what has a history can be changed.

Meanwhile the connection we do have has changed shape. It is networked, individualised, abundant and shallow [[81]](references.md#ref-81 "Wellman, Barry, and Lee Rainie. Networked. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2012."), and the core networks through which people were actually known have thinned [[28]](references.md#ref-28 "McPherson, Miller, Lynn Smith-Lovin, and Matthew E. Brashears. &quot;Social Isolation in America.&quot; American Sociological Review 71, no. 3 (2006): 353–375."). The frontier now is the substitution of the other person altogether: relationships as consumer goods [[90]](references.md#ref-90 "Bauman, Zygmunt. Liquid Love. Cambridge: Polity, 2003.")[[95]](references.md#ref-95 "Illouz, Eva. Why Love Hurts. Cambridge: Polity, 2012.")[[96]](references.md#ref-96 "Zelizer, Viviana A. The Purchase of Intimacy. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2005."), a culture that reduces the resisting other to more of the same [[89]](references.md#ref-89 "Han, Byung-Chul. The Expulsion of the Other. 2016. Trans. Wieland Hoban. Cambridge: Polity, 2018."), and machines that perform attentiveness while never risking refusal [[94]](references.md#ref-94 "Turkle, Sherry. Alone Together. New York: Basic Books, 2011.")[[97]](references.md#ref-97 "Skjuve, Marita, et al. &quot;'My Chatbot Companion': A Study of Human-Chatbot Relationships.&quot; International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 149 (2021): 102601.").

I am using one to write this. That is the whole problem, and I have no cleverer way to put it: I would rather send you a polished essay than risk the unpolished sentence. This letter is the avoidance it describes.

There are two places where I think this argument is load-bearing, and I would rather you broke them than nodded. First: I don't know whether the core of loneliness is being known or *mattering* — counting to others — in which case my central distinction is the wrong one [[17]](references.md#ref-17 "Rosenberg, Morris, and Carrie S. McCullough. &quot;Mattering.&quot; 1981.")[[18]](references.md#ref-18 "Prilleltensky, Isaac, and Ora Prilleltensky. How People Matter. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2021."). Second: the sharpest challenge I found came from Claire Langhamer, who argues that twentieth-century England grew *more* expressive, not less [[40]](references.md#ref-40 "Langhamer, Claire. The English in Love. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2013."). At first this looked fatal — if the emotional register warmed while loneliness worsened, the cause cannot be the register. But I think it dissolves the difficulty rather than compounding it, because expressiveness and disagreeability are different muscles. The twentieth century grew more fluent at confession, feeling and self-revelation while growing less able to hold a position against another person without it becoming a war. The therapeutic and the adversarial are not the same register: we learned to say what we felt, and unlearned how to disagree about it. So Langhamer is right about the warming, and the friction taboo can still be real, because those are different axes.

That is where my thinking has got to. You are my friend, and you would rather disagree with me than spare my feelings — so tell me where it is weak. I would rather have that than a nod.

Joe
